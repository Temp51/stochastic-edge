Kelly Criterion Position Sizing: The Optimal Formula for Bets and Portfolios

Strategy answers "what to buy." Position sizing answers "how much" — and it's the question most investors never formalize. The Kelly Criterion is the mathematically growth-optimal answer, from a single coin flip to a full correlated portfolio.

∞

July 6, 2026 · — min read

Two traders can share an identical edge and reach opposite outcomes. Same signal, same win rate, same expected value per trade. One compounds steadily for a decade. The other blows up in eighteen months. The difference was never the edge. It was how much of the bankroll rode on it each time.

The Kelly Criterion is the formula that answers that question precisely: given a genuine statistical edge, what fraction of capital maximizes long-run compound growth? It was derived in 1956 by a Bell Labs researcher solving a problem that had nothing to do with markets, and it has since become one of the few pieces of gambling theory taken seriously by professional portfolio managers — with real teeth once you extend it past a single bet into a correlated portfolio.

Where This Actually Comes From

John Kelly published A New Interpretation of Information Rate in 1956 — a paper about noisy telephone signal transmission for AT&T, not gambling. Kelly showed that a gambler with private information about the outcome of a bet (a private "signal," in information-theory terms) has a mathematically optimal fraction of bankroll to wager, one that maximizes the long-run exponential growth rate of wealth. Ed Thorp later carried the same math into blackjack card counting and then into running one of the earliest successful quantitative hedge funds. The lineage from information theory to card counting to portfolio management is not a coincidence — it's the same optimization problem wearing three different costumes.

The Single-Bet Formula

Kelly's original setting: a binary bet with win probability p, loss probability q = 1 − p, and payout odds b (a win returns b times the wager; a loss forfeits the wager). The growth-optimal fraction of bankroll to risk is:

f* = (bp − q) / b = p − q/b // Derivation sketch: wealth after n rounds compounds multiplicatively. // Maximizing E[ln(wealth)] — not E[wealth] — is what makes this the // GROWTH-optimal fraction rather than the EV-optimal one. // EV-maximizing would say "bet everything, every time" — and go broke // with probability 1 as soon as q > 0. Log-utility is what saves you.

That last comment is the entire reason Kelly matters. Maximizing expected value of wealth and maximizing expected value of log wealth give wildly different answers once you compound over many rounds. EV-maximization says bet everything on any positive-EV wager. Kelly says something much more conservative — and it's the version that doesn't go to zero.

The Continuous Case: What Investors Actually Use

Markets don't offer discrete win/lose bets with fixed odds — they offer a continuous return distribution. The investing version of Kelly, for a single risky asset with expected excess return μ (over the risk-free rate) and variance σ², reduces to a remarkably clean form:

f* = μ / σ² // Sanity check via Sharpe ratio S = μ/σ: // f* = S/σ — the growth-optimal leverage is your Sharpe ratio // divided by your volatility. Higher edge → size up. // Higher volatility for the SAME edge → size down, fast (σ² in the // denominator means sizing falls off much quicker than edge grows).

Why Full Kelly Is Too Violent to Actually Run

Full Kelly is growth-optimal in expectation, but it is not the same thing as comfortable, or even survivable in practice, for two separate reasons.

Reason one: the growth-vs-variance trade-off is steeply asymmetric near the optimum. Bet a fraction c of the full Kelly size (so c = 1 is full Kelly, c = 0.5 is half-Kelly) and the growth rate you retain, relative to full Kelly's maximum, works out to a clean closed form:

g(c) / g_max = 2c − c² = c(2 − c) // At c = 0.5 (half-Kelly): 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75 → 75% of the growth rate // Variance of the position scales with c², so: // volatility (std dev) at c=0.5 → 50% of full-Kelly volatility // variance at c=0.5 → 25% of full-Kelly variance // // Half-Kelly: give up 1/4 of the growth rate, keep 1/4 of the variance. // This asymmetry is why almost no serious practitioner runs full Kelly.

Reason two, and the more dangerous one in practice: you don't know the true μ. Every formula above assumes you know the real expected return exactly. You don't — you have a sample estimate from historical data, and that estimate is far noisier than most people assume. Here's the derivation, worth doing once so it's not just a rule of thumb:

// Standard error of an ANNUALIZED mean-return estimate, using N years // of daily data (T = 252N days), starting from daily std dev σ_d: SE(daily mean) = σ_d / √T Annualized est. = 252 × (daily mean) SE(annualized est.)= 252 × σ_d/√T = 252σ_d/√(252N) = σ_d√252 / √N SE(μ̂_annual, using N years of data) = σ_annual / √N // Set N = 1: SE(μ̂) = σ_annual. Your ENTIRE one-year return estimate // carries a margin of error as large as annual volatility itself. // With a 20-year backtest: SE = σ_annual / √20 ≈ σ_annual / 4.5 // — still a huge relative error on the number that f* = μ/σ² depends // on LINEARLY. Variance, by contrast, is estimated far more precisely // from the same data. Kelly sizing computed from a backtested μ is // only ever as trustworthy as that noisy numerator.
ℹ
The practical upshot: full Kelly, computed from a backtested edge, is really "full Kelly according to an estimate you should not fully trust." This is the actual, mathematical justification for fractional Kelly — not just risk aversion, but estimation-error hedging.

Fractional Kelly in Practice

Professional users of Kelly sizing — Thorp among the earliest and most explicit about it — typically run somewhere between a quarter and a half of the full Kelly fraction. Given the derivation above, a clean way to think about the choice: pick c low enough that you can survive being meaningfully wrong about μ, since you always are, to some degree.

The Part Most Retail Content Skips: Multi-Asset Kelly

Almost every accessible explanation of Kelly stops at a single bet or a single asset. It doesn't stop there in practice — real portfolios hold many correlated positions, and correlation changes the optimal sizing substantially. The extension replaces scalar division with a matrix inversion:

w* = Σ⁻¹ (μ − r_f · 1) Where: w* = vector of growth-optimal portfolio weights, one per asset Σ = covariance matrix of asset returns (n × n) μ = vector of expected returns for each asset r_f = risk-free rate 1 = vector of ones (same length as μ) // This is not a coincidence or approximation: the growth-optimal Kelly // portfolio is IDENTICAL to the maximum-Sharpe-ratio portfolio from // classical mean-variance optimization — just at a specific leverage. // Two different 20th-century derivations (Markowitz 1952, Kelly via // Kelly 1956 / Latané 1959) land on the same portfolio direction.

The inverse covariance matrix is doing something specific and non-obvious: it accounts for how each asset's risk diversifies against the others. An asset with a mediocre or even negative expected return can still receive a positive Kelly weight if it reduces total portfolio variance enough — the formula sizes the portfolio as a system, not as a sum of independently-sized bets. Sizing each asset independently with the single-asset formula and ignoring correlation is one of the most common — and most costly — simplifications in practice.

Full Python Implementation

Both the single-asset and multi-asset versions, with fractional scaling built in:

python 
import numpy as np


def kelly_single_asset(mu: float, sigma: float, kelly_fraction: float = 1.0) -> float:
    """
    Growth-optimal position size for a single risky asset.

    mu             : expected EXCESS return (over risk-free), annualized, decimal
    sigma          : annualized volatility (std dev), decimal
    kelly_fraction : scaling factor c. c=1.0 is full Kelly, c=0.5 is half-Kelly.

    Returns the fraction of capital to allocate (can exceed 1.0 = leverage).
    """
    full_kelly = mu / (sigma ** 2)
    return kelly_fraction * full_kelly


def kelly_multi_asset(
    mu: np.ndarray,
    cov: np.ndarray,
    risk_free: float = 0.0,
    kelly_fraction: float = 0.5,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Growth-optimal portfolio weights across N correlated assets.

    mu             : (N,) array of annualized expected returns
    cov            : (N, N) annualized covariance matrix of returns
    risk_free      : risk-free rate, annualized decimal
    kelly_fraction : scaling factor c, applied uniformly across all weights

    Returns an (N,) array of portfolio weights. Weights can be negative
    (short) or sum to more than 1.0 (leverage) — that is correct Kelly
    behavior, not a bug. Clip/normalize externally if your broker or
    risk framework requires it.
    """
    excess = mu - risk_free
    cov_inv = np.linalg.inv(cov)
    full_kelly_weights = cov_inv @ excess
    return kelly_fraction * full_kelly_weights


# ── Example: single asset ─────────────────────────────────────────────
f_full = kelly_single_asset(mu=0.08, sigma=0.20, kelly_fraction=1.0)
f_half = kelly_single_asset(mu=0.08, sigma=0.20, kelly_fraction=0.5)
print(f"Full Kelly:  {f_full:.2%} of capital")
print(f"Half Kelly:  {f_half:.2%} of capital")

# ── Example: three correlated assets ──────────────────────────────────
mu  = np.array([0.09, 0.06, 0.04])            # expected annual returns
vol = np.array([0.22, 0.15, 0.08])            # annual volatilities
corr = np.array([                            # correlation matrix
    [1.00, 0.40, 0.10],
    [0.40, 1.00, 0.05],
    [0.10, 0.05, 1.00],
])
cov = np.outer(vol, vol) * corr              # build covariance from vol + correlation

weights = kelly_multi_asset(mu, cov, risk_free=0.04, kelly_fraction=0.5)
for i, w in enumerate(weights):
    print(f"Asset {i+1}: {w:.1%} of capital")
print(f"Total leverage: {weights.sum():.2f}x")

Where This Breaks — Said Plainly

  • Kelly assumes you know your edge. As shown above, you almost never know it precisely. Every output of this formula is only as good as the μ estimate feeding it.
  • Kelly assumes stationarity. The formula optimizes for a return distribution that stays constant. Real markets shift regimes — which is exactly the subject of the next article in this series.
  • Kelly ignores transaction costs, taxes, and borrowing constraints. The leverage a covariance matrix recommends may not be available to you, or may not survive real-world frictions once you try to implement it.
  • Full Kelly's drawdowns are brutal even when the math is followed perfectly. A well-known property of growth-optimal betting: you should expect to see paper losses of 50%+ from a peak at some point, even when every assumption holds exactly. This is not a sign the strategy failed — it's the strategy working as designed. Most people cannot stomach that in practice, which is the real-world argument for fractional Kelly that no formula captures.

Key Takeaways

  1. Kelly maximizes E[log wealth], not E[wealth] — that single substitution is the entire reason the formula avoids ruin where naive EV-maximizing bet sizing does not.
  2. Half-Kelly gives up 25% of the growth rate for 75% less variance — a genuinely favorable trade for almost anyone, derived above rather than asserted.
  3. Estimation error in μ, not the formula itself, is the real danger of running full Kelly — your edge is never known as precisely as the math assumes.
  4. Correlation changes everything once you move past a single bet — size the portfolio as a system via the covariance matrix, not asset-by-asset.
